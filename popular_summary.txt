In quantum computers that protect information against errors, some operations require costly resources called magic states. We show how a compiler's decision to act early can commit these resources in a model that translates rotations separately, without extra working qubits. If a rotation arrives in pieces, the compiler can remember them until their sum is known or issue instructions that cannot be withdrawn. Even when the pieces eventually cancel completely, early instructions can remain costly.

By connecting information theory with the structure of quantum gate sequences, we place quantitative limits on this trade between retained information and committed operations. The result concerns information carried between input rounds, rather than the controller's physical storage capacity. It applies to a compiler that must handle all permitted inputs without knowing beforehand that cancellation will occur. Allowing extra working qubits and processing several rotations together can change the conclusion. The work identifies when postponing compilation preserves opportunities to save quantum resources.
